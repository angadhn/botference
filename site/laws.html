The laws of
publishing a blyg.

When botference publishes a blyg for its owner, it makes promises. This page writes each promise as one sentence, says where it comes from, and says how it is checked today. Every law here is a proposal. Only the owner can adopt one.

Why laws

The Blygger project explains this idea in “How Blygger Studio is tested now, and why there is more test code than product code”. A law is “one sentence, in plain words, that starts with ‘For every…’”, and it cites where its promise comes from. Each law is then checked by a small, independent model of what should happen, against generated sequences of actions. Small bugs are planted in the product, one at a time, to prove that some law fails for each of them. The post credits that oracle framework to Kyle Mathews.

People own the laws. … An AI may propose a law; it may not adopt or change one.

The same post says that a Blygger client does not need any of this: “The protocol asks only that what you publish conforms.” So botference does not need laws to be a Blygger client. It wants them for a different reason.

AI models write most of botference's code. This week, independent reviews found real faults again and again, and the tests that the authoring model wrote had passed every time. In one fault, a queued press could publish an older text of a comment than one the owner had already pressed. In another, an agent's text could carry hidden HTML attributes into the published page. Some reviews also planted small faults in the code and found tests that did not notice them.

A test that the same model writes from the same code shares that model's blind spots. A law is written from the promise, not from the code, and a person decides whether it is the right promise. That is the check botference does not have yet.

The laws

Authority is where the promise comes from: a section of frontends/plugin/SPEC.md with its date, the CHANGELOG, or the Blygger protocol. Checked today names test files that exist now. They test chosen examples. None of them uses an independent model, generated sequences of actions, or planted bugs. Some tests are in the owner's own site (a Jekyll site), not in this repository; the law says so.

How a law is adopted

  • The owner reads each law, then adopts it, edits it, or rejects it. An AI does none of these.
  • An adopted law then gets an independent check. The check is written from the law, not from the code.
  • A law that changes later changes the same way: the owner decides, and the date is recorded.
